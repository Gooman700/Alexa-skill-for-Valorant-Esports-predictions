\section{Model Results}
In accordance with the evaluation strategies established in \autoref{chap:req_analysis}, the LightGBM model was tuned and tested against a 70\% training set, a 20\% hold-out testing set, and a 10\% final validation set. The model was evaluated against the testing set upon each hyperparameter tweak, with the goal of reaching the highest accuracy possible. The purpose of the validation set was to provide a final evaluation of the model on completely unseen data, ensuring that it has not been overfitted to the testing set.

Upon the model reaching 59.84\% accuracy on the testing data, it was finally evaluated on the validation data, where the Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 Score were calculated, alongside a confusion matrix:

\section{Alexa Skill Results}
Adhering to the evaluation strategies established in \autoref{chap:req_analysis}, the Alexa Skill's execution time and resolved utterances were monitored through the use of AWS CloudWatch, providing insight into the performance of the skill. As seen in \autoref{fig:latency} below, the P50 (or median) response time was 1053ms, just over one second. The P90 (slower than average) reponse time was 1478ms. These values indicate that the Alexa Skill resolves the user's utterance, executes the Lambda Function, and returns as response, all in under two seconds, which comfortably remains below both the eight second limit, and the six second requirement.

Additionally, utterances being resolved, and their confidence, were recorded and plotted. \autoref{fig:utterancesResolved} depicts the abundance of high confidence utterances. This shows that the interaction model contains a broad range of sample utterances which successfully capture the natural language associated with activating the \texttt{MatchPredictionIntent}.

\section{User Evaluation}
\subsection{The Questionnaire}
Following system implementation, a user evaluation questionnaire was distributed to various Universities' Valorant Discord servers along with some background information on the project. The first page of the questionnaire contained a consent form which needed to be filled before the following pages could be viewed. The entire questionnaire can be seen in \autoref{app:questionnairePages}. Additionally, both the application for, and the approval of, research ethics can be seen in \autoref{app:questionnaire_application}.

The purpose of the questionnaire was to gather target users' likes, dislikes, and suggestions for the skill through the use of 5-point Likert Scales and free text responses. The 5-point Likert Scales ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

\subsection{Questionnaire Results}
The reponses for each 5-point Likert Scale question were consolidated into corresponding pie charts, as depicted in \autoref{fig:questionnaireResults} below. Notably, out of the eight participants, all responses were either a four or a five---or a somewhat agree and strongly agree. This suggests that the skill appeals to the intended users, providing utility and/or entertainment.

One such response that all participants agreed upon was that `Alexa prompts [them] in a way [they] understand when something went wrong'. This reponse indicates that the slot validation implemented in \autoref{sub:slotValidation} works well, and the spoken responses provide relevant details to the user.

Furthermore, the free text responses enabled the participants to elucidate their personal thoughts on the skill. Key insights provided by the free text responses are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Participants agreed that prompts were descriptive and easy to follow.
    \item One participant appreciated that the skill doesn't constantly require confirmations (e.g., `I heard that you wanted to make a prediction for NRG and Sentinels, is that correct?').
    \item Participants mentioned that the skill can sometimes get confused between teams with similar names, especially when referring to them by a nickname.
    \item One participant suggested implementing the ability to ask for a team lineup.
\end{itemize}

To improve the interaction model's ability to resolve a team name correctly, additional synonyms would need to be included within the `team' slot type's values.

\subsection{Reliability of the Data}
Using Cochran's formula as outlined by Kotrlik\cite{kotrlik2001organizational}, the target sample size for a 95\% confidence level with a 5\% margin of error was calculated to be 385 participants. However, due to the logistical constraints of beta-testing an unpublished skill, the sample size reached only $N=8$. At a 95\% confidence level, this yields a margin of error of $\approx$ 35\%, meaning these results represent preliminary trends rather than definitive population metrics. Nonetheless, 100\% of participants exhibited positive sentiment towards the utility of the skill.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:futureWork}
The final product fulfilled this project's goal of creating a voice-activated machine learning model capable of predicting the outcome of VCT matches. Even so, the routes opened up by the work completed here leave much to expand upon, with potential to transition the VCT Predictor into a VCT Assistant; for example, the skill could be equipped with further functionality, more intents, and integration with external applications.

The first priority would be to expand upon the list of synonyms attributed to each team, reducing the number of mis-resolved teams by increasing the coverage of individual nicknames. Secondly, extra intents building upon the existing data would be implemented to provide a richer user experience; for example, the ability to request a team's historical results as stated in \autoref{tab:requirements_evaluation} \#12. Finally, external services would be integrated into the skill to enable real-time updates on game statistics. Multiple API endpoints are available online which can be queried to receive a JSON object of in-progess VCT games, of which the corresponding data can be extracted and utilised to enhance the model's predictions of these games.
